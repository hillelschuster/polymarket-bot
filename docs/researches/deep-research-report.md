# Polymarket Alpha Memo

## What I learned from the repository

I audited the repository first, and the right takeaway is not “copy profitable wallets.” It is “only trade signal that still has executable upside after you arrive.” The bot discovers candidates from multi-horizon and category leaderboards, refreshes hotter wallets more often, aggregates split fills into 10-minute campaigns, scores wallets on ROI, consistency, copyability, and category edge, then applies downstream market gates before a trade is admitted. Most importantly, it refuses to use midpoint fantasy: every admitted copy needs a real CLOB buy quote, open PnL is marked to executable sell quotes, and final PnL is booked only once the market is officially closed and terminal. That is exactly the right style for new research: small rule count, real books, honest fills, honest marks, and clear separation between **finding** signal and **capturing** it. citeturn4view0turn4view1turn17view1turn15view1turn18view0turn18view1

The repo also matters for what **not** to waste time on. It already has explicit research inventory for political favorites, simple calendar monotonicity, and post-final sports lag, and its notes are blunt that sports and crypto are usually efficient at short horizons absent a non-market signal. So the best new work is not “more architecture” and not “generic in-game sports arb”; it is structural edges where Polymarket’s plumbing, timing, fee curve, resolution mechanics, or wallet transparency leaves money on the table. citeturn4view1turn19view0turn19view1turn19view2

## Ranking at a glance

My ranking, by expected edge multiplied by feasibility, is: **macro release latency**, **fee-adjusted complement and basket dislocations**, **specialist wallets that never show up on the leaderboard**, **rules-text and resolution-source basis trades**, **cross-market semantic dependency arbitrage outside bot-heavy sports**, **display-price illusion fades in wide-spread books**, and **five-minute crypto settlement-reversal trades**. I am explicitly cutting “generic NBA in-game arbitrage” from the list because the best current evidence says Polymarket NBA books are already very tight: only 7 executable single-market anomalies were found across 3,042 markets, with a 3.6-second median duration, and even the richer combinatorial trades were mostly constrained to about 14.8 shares on average. That is real money only at retail scale and only if you are already playing a speed game. citeturn22view0turn23view1

## The best ideas

**Macro release latency against faster external markets.** **Mechanism:** Polymarket macro and economics contracts appear to react more slowly than the fastest external price-discovery venues around discrete releases, so the loser is the trader staring at Polymarket itself instead of the upstream signal. **Why it exists:** macro contracts are episodic, retail-heavy, and often thin; many participants do not translate Treasury, Fed-funds, CPI, or BLS-release information into contract probabilities in real time. **Evidence:** the strongest current evidence is Aktuğ and Torul’s “informational inertia” result from the May 2024 CPI leak: CME 30-Day Fed Funds futures moved within seconds, while Polymarket’s Fed rate-cut contracts showed a near-zero top-of-book response for about 35 minutes, with the authors narrowing the behavioral claim to the post-public-release segment; Polymarket also provides public real-time feeds and historical market/trade reads, so this is cheap to verify live. **How to capture it:** build a scheduled-release monitor for CPI, NFP, FOMC, unemployment, GDP, rate decisions, and major crypto-proxy macro prints; compute fair probabilities from faster markets or official surprise sign; then either cross stale Polymarket books immediately or rest maker quotes once the move starts but before the crowd finishes repricing. **Risks:** this is lumpy, not continuous; big calendar events will attract more competition over time; fee schedules matter on some categories; and the best windows may now be minutes, not 35 minutes. **Rough edge estimate:** about 1%–5% of deployed notional on good events, occasionally much higher on leak-like shocks; that estimate is an inference from the documented lag, reduced for current fees and slippage. **Fastest experiment:** record every relevant Polymarket macro book from two minutes before to fifteen minutes after the next CPI and FOMC releases, then compare executable Polymarket movement with CME/Fed-funds movement and mark the fillable gap, not the midpoint gap. citeturn26search13turn26search16turn26search5turn33view4turn37search1turn37search6

**Fee-adjusted complement and multi-outcome underround scanner.** **Mechanism:** Polymarket’s theoretical parity relationships are simple, but the executable relationships are not, because books are separate, fees vary with price, and UI prices are not executable prices. The loser is the trader comparing displayed prices instead of all-in and all-out prices. **Why it exists:** complementary YES/NO books and multi-outcome markets get traded one leg at a time by humans; fees are symmetric around 50% and bite hardest near the center; and non-atomic execution means many traders ignore or cannot cleanly close these gaps. **Evidence:** Polymarket’s docs say prices emerge from the CLOB, displayed prices are midpoint or, when spread is wide, sometimes the last trade, and matched complementary orders imply a $1 split in theory; the fee docs show the actual dollar fee curve by price and category; Saguillo et al. formalize arbitrage when sums of YES prices fall below or rise above logical bounds; Cheng et al. find combinatorial Polymarket NBA arbitrage with a 101 bps median yield, but shallow depth caps size. **How to capture it:** do exactly what the repo does for wallet copy, but for baskets: compute fee-adjusted executable buy and sell costs from the live books, demand at least 70–100 bps net edge after all fees and a one-tick safety margin, and prefer non-NBA, non-headline categories where bots are thinner. **Risks:** partial fills are the whole game, not a footnote; shallow size can strand one leg; and if Polymarket’s combo tooling expands, part of this edge compresses. **Rough edge estimate:** roughly 0.5%–2% gross per basket, mostly retail-scale, with more frequency than most people think and less scalable size than most people want. **Fastest experiment:** run a seven-day scanner over all active binary complements and multi-outcome baskets, using real executable books and current fee schedules, and count only opportunities that persist longer than two seconds and support at least $50 notional. citeturn34view0turn33view0turn33view1turn23view3turn22view0turn23view1turn33view4

**Specialist-wallet mining outside the leaderboard.** **Mechanism:** the next wallet edge is probably not “top PnL wallet on the leaderboard”; it is likely a low-frequency specialist with real domain edge in a narrow category where the books are still copyable. The loser is the broad market participant treating weather, niche political process, quirky rules markets, or specialized NO-side trading as entertainment. **Why it exists:** leaderboards overweight scale and can hide small but strong specialists; a wallet taking two or three high-conviction positions a month can have much richer per-trade edge than a top-volume address. **Evidence:** the repo itself is still leaderboard-led and explicitly treats adjacent categories like weather as only shadow-copy candidates; Leo Labs’ reverse-engineering of 40 profitable wallets found a separate “knowledge-based” class trading infrequently but with deep category specialization, including weather and persistent NO specialists; and Polymarket now exposes public wallet activity and public trades, while PMXT v1 made full-life-cycle search materially cheaper by releasing 1.2 billion ground-truth trades. **How to capture it:** mine wallets that are **not** in the top discovery scopes but have high category concentration, long hold durations, redeem-heavy exits, positive realized edge after executable-entry assumptions, and low overlap with obvious copy crowds; then shadow-copy tiny and promote only after a fresh out-of-sample sample. **Risks:** sparse samples, survivorship bias, hidden private information, and rapid decay once copied. **Rough edge estimate:** 5%–15% expected ROI per position for the best specialists, but at very low frequency. **Fastest experiment:** use PMXT plus live public wallet activity to rank fifty non-leaderboard specialists, then paper-follow their next twenty live entries using the repo’s executable-entry discipline and compare them with blind leaderboard copies. citeturn4view1turn38view0turn37search3turn37search4turn36view0turn36view1

**Rules-text and resolution-source basis trades.** **Mechanism:** markets get traded on the headline, but they settle on the rules. The loser is the trader betting the news story without reading the resolution source, the deadline, or the edge-case language. **Why it exists:** rules reading is boring, slow, and under-rewarded socially; headline chasing is easy. In practice, that means many traders price “what seems true,” not “what will actually pay.” **Evidence:** Polymarket’s own docs say the title is not enough and that markets resolve using pre-defined rules covering source, end date, and edge cases; the resolution process itself can take two hours or much longer if disputed through UMA; Polymarket US sports FAQs explicitly say settlement is delayed if the official result is under review and, if no official result is declared by expiry, settlement falls back to last fair market prices; and recent information-leakage research found that resolution semantics, not just data availability, are the main bottleneck in identifying informed or ambiguous cases at scale. **How to capture it:** build a parser that flags markets with fragile wording—“official announcement,” named websites, hard date cutoffs, “by” versus “on,” metric definitions, or fallback fair-price language—then trade **after** the news shock if the move is inconsistent with the rule text. **Risks:** low frequency, capital can get tied up during dispute windows, and some markets are genuinely ambiguous. **Rough edge estimate:** 5%–30% on the good ones, but maybe only a few times per quarter. **Fastest experiment:** manually track the fifty highest-volume breaking-news markets for one month, snapshot the price before and after major headlines, and mark whether the rule-consistent side or the headline-consistent side won the move. citeturn30search0turn39search0turn41search1turn36view3

**Cross-market semantic dependency arbitrage outside bot-heavy sports.** **Mechanism:** some markets are not complements, but they are still logically linked: later-date YES should not be cheaper than earlier-date YES; “wins party nomination” and “wins presidency” have inequality constraints; “wins” and “wins by 2+” are implication-linked; threshold ladders and deadline ladders should respect monotonicity. The loser is the trader looking at one market at a time. **Why it exists:** Polymarket’s fragmented CLOB makes cross-market parity a manual cognitive task, and non-atomic fill risk keeps many people out even when the logic is obvious. **Evidence:** Saguillo et al. show that arbitrage on Polymarket exists not only within markets but also across dependent markets, and they formalize those dependencies; Cheng et al. show NBA is already heavily arbitraged, which is exactly why the better hunting ground is outside the most bot-defended live-sports set; the repo’s research history already includes a narrow calendar-monotonicity lane, which validates the shape of the edge but not the full opportunity set. **How to capture it:** do **not** build a giant ontology; define a handful of deterministic templates by category—deadline ladders, threshold ladders, winner-versus-party, wins-versus-margin, “before date A” versus “before date B”—and trigger only when the inequality breaks by more than fees, one tick, and execution slack. **Risks:** semantic false positives, low capacity, and nasty one-leg risk if the book disappears mid-basket. **Rough edge estimate:** 1%–5% gross per basket, usually better persistence than same-market parity errors because fewer people watch them. **Fastest experiment:** scan all current Gamma markets for thirty days using just five template families, manually review the top hundred violations, and measure how many survive a thirty-second persistence filter. citeturn26search14turn22view1turn23view2turn23view3turn22view0turn23view1turn4view1

**Display-price illusion in wide-spread books.** **Mechanism:** the number people mentally anchor to on Polymarket is often not tradable. When markets are wide, the UI can show midpoint or even last trade rather than the price you will actually pay or receive. The loser is the trader buying the screen instead of the book. **Why it exists:** retail traders think in “probability moved to 62%,” but execution happens at best ask or best bid and sometimes far worse in thin depth. This gets nastiest in longshots and niche categories. **Evidence:** Polymarket’s docs explicitly say displayed price is the midpoint of the bid-ask spread and, when the spread is wider than $0.10, the last traded price is shown instead; the repo is explicit that Gamma midpoint is never a valid entry and that executable CLOB prices are the only trustworthy marks; Dubach documents a longshot spread premium and shows that microstructure cost meaningfully shapes what information survives into price. **How to capture it:** treat large displayed-price moves in wide-spread markets as a behavioral flow signal, not as information. Fade them with passive offers or by buying the other side when executable mid/all-in barely moved, especially in weather, culture, mentions, and other thin books. **Risks:** some “fake” moves are actually the first print of real news; maker inventory can sit too long; and this edge is better for patient small-capital quoting than for impatient takers. **Rough edge estimate:** 2–6 cents per share on the best fills, often translating to several percent on capital per completed round trip. **Fastest experiment:** backtest a simple rule on live books: spread above 8 cents, displayed move exceeds executable-mid move by more than 4 cents, then measure one-hour and twenty-four-hour reversion. citeturn34view0turn14view0turn18view1turn24view0

**Five-minute crypto settlement-reversal trades.** **Mechanism:** very short crypto contracts can be manipulated at settlement because the underlying is itself tradable; the trader who chases the final push is the one donating. **Why it exists:** settlement horizons can be short enough that moving the underlying is rational for a manipulator, while retail still treats the last seconds as informative. **Evidence:** Dai, Jia, and Yu find settlement-time spot order flow spikes and large price reversals after settlement in Polymarket’s five-minute Bitcoin contract, and they find the problem largely absent in fifteen-minute contracts; Polymarket’s predictions changelog says taker fees were enabled on fifteen-minute crypto markets and that five-minute crypto markets launched with the same fee curve, which is exactly what you would expect if short-horizon latency/manipulation needed to be taxed. **How to capture it:** never be the one buying the last-second move. Either trade the immediate reversal after settlement using the next linked contract as the expression vehicle, or only trade fifteen-minute crypto and use five-minute close distortions as a contra indicator. **Risks:** this is the fastest-decaying edge in the memo, fees are real, and execution can become a speed battle. **Rough edge estimate:** about 0.5%–1.5% net per clean cycle if you are selective and do not pay away the spread twice. **Fastest experiment:** replay one thousand recent BTC five-minute windows and measure reversal from settlement to +30 seconds and +2 minutes, then compare that with the next contract’s price and fee burden. citeturn20search1turn20search13turn28search9turn33view0

## What I would build first

If I were starting tomorrow, I would not spread effort across seven ideas evenly. I would build **two live monitors first**: the macro-release latency monitor and the fee-adjusted basket scanner. They are the cleanest structural edges, they do not rely on secret information, they can be verified quickly with public feeds, and they fit the repo’s style of “admit only what is executable now.” I would use PMXT v1 only as the historical spine and keep the live layer simple: public market stream, public trades, executable book snapshots, and a small journal with entry, expected value, fillability, and realized convergence. citeturn33view4turn37search1turn37search4turn36view0turn36view1turn15view1

The third build would be a **specialist-wallet miner**, not a generic copy bot. The ranking logic should explicitly prefer category concentration, redeem-heavy behavior, low crowd overlap, and executable post-entry upside over raw leaderboard fame. In parallel, I would run a manual **rules-basis blotter** because the best rules-text trades are infrequent but fat-tailed, and they often need human reading before they need code. citeturn4view1turn38view0turn37search3turn37search4

## Bottom line

The best edge here is probably **not** another version of blind wallet copying. The two highest-conviction opportunities are structural: **Polymarket can still be slow to absorb discrete macro information**, and **its executable books can still violate fee-adjusted logical parity in ways that are too small for institutions and plenty large for a disciplined retail bot**. The most underappreciated non-structural angle is **specialist wallets that never rank high enough to attract copy crowds**. The weirdest but very real corner is **rules-text basis**: markets settle on text, not vibes. If you force all of these through the repo’s standard—real books, realistic fills, honest PnL—you have a research plan with actual money in front of it instead of theory. citeturn26search13turn22view0turn36view0turn30search0turn15view1
